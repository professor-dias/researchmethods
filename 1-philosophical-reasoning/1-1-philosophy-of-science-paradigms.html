How Do You Know What You Know?

Before we get into the mechanics of research — designs, statistics, ethics — it's worth pausing on a more basic question: how do you decide something is true in the first place? That's what this whole page is about, and it's the foundation everything else in this course builds on.

Humans have been answering that question for thousands of years, long before "the scientific method" existed. Most people still mix all four ways of knowing below without noticing — tap one to see it in action.

Notice the pattern: authority, tenacity, and logic can all hand you an idea worth testing — but only scientific inquiry insists that the idea survive contact with real, collectable data. That's not a coincidence, and it's not just history. It's what the rest of this page explores — starting with why authority and tradition still need to be checked, right now, not just in Galileo's time.

Why Authority Still Needs to Be Critiqued — Even Now

The Galileo story above can feel like ancient history — surely we're past trusting an idea just because an authority backed it? We aren't. A credentialed expert, a trusted institution, a regulator's approval — perceived authority still shapes what gets believed and acted on, and it has caused real, well-documented harm well within living memory.

💊
The Opioid Crisis
A pharmaceutical company, backed by physicians' prescribing authority and regulatory approval, assured patients and doctors that a powerful opioid carried a low risk of addiction. That reassurance — resting on authority, not adequate evidence — helped fuel a public health crisis that has cost hundreds of thousands of lives.
🫻
Theranos
A company built almost entirely on perceived authority — a charismatic founder, a board of prominent former officials, glowing press coverage — claimed its technology could run hundreds of medical tests from a single drop of blood. It couldn't. Patients received inaccurate results about their own health before the fraud was exposed.
💉
The 1998 Vaccine–Autism Study
A single study, published in a respected journal by a credentialed physician, claimed a link between a childhood vaccine and autism. The paper was later retracted and its methods discredited — but the damage from that borrowed authority persists in vaccination rates today.
Notice what all three have in common: none of them required the underlying claim to survive real scrutiny before people acted on it. The credential, the institution, or the publication did the convincing — not the evidence.
What other examples can you think of? Somewhere between historical and current events, authority — political, medical, religious, institutional — has convinced large numbers of people of something that later turned out to be false or harmful. Where have you seen it?

Tenacity, Tradition & Critical Thinking

Tenacity is the most invisible way of knowing, because it rarely announces itself as a belief at all — it just feels like "how things are." That makes it worth examining with the same rigor as any other claim, especially when a tradition causes real harm.

Case study: Female genital mutilation (FGM). FGM — the partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons — is still practiced in parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, almost always justified as tradition or a rite of passage. The World Health Organization classifies it as a violation of human rights: it provides no health benefit, causes serious and often lifelong physical and psychological harm, and its persistence rests entirely on social tradition and pressure to conform — not on any medical or scientific rationale.
If a practice this harmful can persist for generations purely on the strength of "this is how it's always been done," what does that tell you about how carefully that phrase needs to be examined — even for beliefs that feel far more harmless?

Logic & Reasoning and the Fallacy Trap

Logic feels different from authority and tenacity — it feels earned, not just asserted. But logic has its own blind spot: a fallacy is a specific, well-documented flaw in the structure of an argument, one that can make a false conclusion feel just as convincing as a true one. Logic only checks whether a conclusion follows from its premises — it never checks whether the premises, or the reasoning connecting them, are actually sound.

What Makes Scientific Inquiry Different?

Scientific inquiry isn't just "being extra careful." It's built from four specific ingredients — and if any one of them is missing, what you have is closer to logic or tenacity wearing a lab coat.

Empiricism
Knowledge has to come from observation, not just from thinking hard about it. You collect data instead of reasoning your way to an answer from your armchair.
Data
Concrete facts collected to answer a specific question — qualitative (differ in kind, like eye color) or quantitative (differ in degree, like height).
Theory
Not a guess — an organized explanation of why a set of observations hang together. We build a whole page (1.3) around this one.
Falsification
A real scientific claim has to be provably wrong-able. If no observation could ever refute it, it isn't a scientific claim — it's a belief.
Philosopher Karl Popper put it sharply: "All monkeys like bananas" is scientific — you could find one monkey that hates them and disprove it. "God exists" is not — there's no observation that could settle it either way. Falsifiability is the line between the two.
Scientific inquiry just won the comparison — it's the only way of knowing that requires an idea to survive an actual attempt to disprove it. But "do science" isn't one single instruction. Two researchers can both be rigorously empirical, both demand falsifiable claims, and still disagree completely about what counts as real evidence in the first place. That disagreement has a name.

Paradigms: The Lens Your Field Hands You

Sociologist Thomas Kuhn popularized the word paradigm — from the Greek paradeigma, "model" or "pattern." A paradigm is a discipline's shared worldview: which questions count, which methods are trustworthy, what "good evidence" even looks like. Change the paradigm, and you change what the whole field considers obvious.

Health research mostly runs on two paradigms, and you'll recognize them immediately — they're the philosophical roots of Topics 3 and 5 later in this course.

Neither paradigm is "more scientific" than the other — they answer different kinds of questions. "Does the drug lower blood pressure?" needs positivism. "Why do patients stop taking it?" needs interpretivism. Most real health research eventually needs both, which is exactly what Mixed Methods is for.
Two more worldviews you'll see in the literature 

Later researchers split these two further. Postpositivism softens strict positivism — it admits that even careful measurement is never perfectly objective, so we can only approximate truth, never prove it outright. Pragmatism sits deliberately in between: instead of starting from a worldview and picking methods to match, it starts from the research question and uses whatever combination of methods actually answers it — the philosophical backbone of mixed-methods research.
