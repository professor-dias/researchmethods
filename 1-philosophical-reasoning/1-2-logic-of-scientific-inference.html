Two Ways of Scientific Inquiry

1.1 ended with scientific inquiry winning the comparison — the one way of knowing that requires an idea to survive an attempt to disprove it. But "doing science" splits into two distinct directions of travel. Watch these two pictures come into focus — they'll show you the difference better than any definition can.

Inductive: watching the picture reveal itself

What do you think this is? Bring it into focus a little at a time.

It's a dog! At what point did you know?

That's exploratory analysis. You revealed information gradually and formed an understanding — without testing any specific claim.

Deductive: naming your guess before the picture clears

H1: This animal is a dog.

State the hypothesis first — then we'll gather evidence.

That's deductive reasoning. You named your best guess before all the evidence was in, then let the evidence confirm or reject it — this time, it didn't survive.

Putting words to what you just saw

Both approaches can involve a hypothesis. The real difference is direction (data-first vs. theory-first) and when the hypothesis shows up.

Inductive (bottom-up)Deductive (top-down)
Starting pointSpecific observations / dataA theory, prior knowledge, or a specific hypothesis
Typical question"What patterns exist in this data?""Does the data support this specific claim?"
Role of a hypothesisMay generate a hypothesis as an output (hypothesis-generating)Starts from a hypothesis and tests it against new data (hypothesis-testing)
GoalDiscover / describeConfirm / evaluate
Typical outputsProbabilities, summaries, patternsEffect size, confidence interval, p-value, significance decision

The Mystery Jar

Time to actually do both kinds of reasoning, not just watch them. Somewhere behind the scenes sits a jar of blue and gold marbles in a fixed, hidden ratio. Pick a mode below and try it yourself.

Draw First, Conclude Later

No hypothesis yet — just start drawing marbles (one at a time, replaced each time) and watch the pattern build. When you feel confident, reveal the jar's true makeup and see how close your read was.

0 marbles drawn

That's inductive reasoning: you had no hypothesis going in — you let the data build a generalization for you, one draw at a time.

Hypothesis First, Test It Against Data

H1: This jar is mostly blue — about 70% blue, 30% gold.

State the hypothesis first, predict what it implies, then gather evidence.

Notice the two panels used the same kind of evidence — drawn marbles, tallied and charted — but arrived at it from opposite directions. That back-and-forth between observation and theory is exactly what 1.3's Wheel of Science is built around.
