If Science Were a Movie, Theory Would Be the Screenplay

Even the best actors need a script to make their performance mean something. In research, your data are the actors — theory is what turns their performance into a coherent story.

People often use theory and hypothesis as if they were the same thing. They are not.

HypothesisTheory
ScopeOne specific, testable statementAn organized set of relationships that explains a whole pattern
Example"Obesity is related to higher breast cancer risk in postmenopausal women"A framework describing how physiological, environmental, and psychological factors interact to produce that risk
JobGets tested and either supported or rejectedGuides which hypotheses are worth testing in the first place
A single hypothesis is one sentence. A theory is the reason that sentence made sense to write down at all.

From Fuzzy Idea to Measurable Number

Every theory is built out of things you cannot directly touch — "stress," "motivation," "trust." Turning a fuzzy idea into something you can actually collect data on takes three steps.

Concept
"Stress" — a broad, abstract idea
Construct
"Perceived stress" — the concept, narrowed to something a theory can use
Variable
Score on the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale — something you can actually measure
This is the same ladder you will climb yourself in Topic 2, when you build your own research strategy: every variable you ever measure started out as somebody's fuzzy idea.

Two Ways to Build a Theory

Long before "the scientific method" was a phrase, two competing strategies emerged for building theory — and researchers still use both today, often without naming them.

Baconian Inductivism (Francis Bacon, 1600s)
1. Observe and classify relevant facts
2. Generalize using inductive reasoning
3. Build a framework that lets you deduce predictions
4. Verify the predictions
Weakness: needs a large number of observations, and cannot theorize about things nobody can observe.
Hypothetico-Deductivism (Isaac Newton, 1600s)
1. Start from a hypothesis or general principle
2. Deduce a specific, testable prediction from it
3. Test the prediction against real observation
4. Verify, revise, or reject the hypothesis
Weakness: the same data can sometimes support more than one competing theory.
Which one did early AIDS research actually use? 

In the early 1980s, physicians in several cities independently noticed rare infections appearing in young gay men. There was no existing theory to deduce from — so researchers had no choice but to start with Baconian inductivism: observe the pattern first, then generalize. That produced a flood of competing early hypotheses (a virus, drug use, an existing infection, even swine fever) that were only narrowed down once each one was tested against new patient data — at which point the process shifted into hypothetico-deductivism.

What You've Found So Far

Moving Theory → Hypothesis → Observation is deduction. Moving Observation → Generalization → back to Theory is induction. This is exactly the same inductive/deductive split from Topic 1.2 — just zoomed out to the scale of an entire research program.

The wheel has no official starting point. A researcher can enter at Theory (deductive) or enter at Observation (inductive) — and if a study gets stuck, sometimes the fix is to travel the wheel backward instead of forward.

What Kind of Explanation Is It?

Not every theory tries to answer the same question. Most health-behavior theories are phenomenological — and that is a deliberate, practical choice, not a weaker one.

Phenomenological
Describes and generalizes a pattern without claiming to explain the underlying cause.
Example: The Health Belief Model — it describes how perceived risk and perceived benefit relate to health behavior, without claiming to explain the deep psychological mechanism behind perception itself.
Explanatory
Identifies the actual causal mechanism — the why, not just the pattern.
Example: General relativity does not just describe that objects fall — it explains gravity as a distortion in space-time itself.
