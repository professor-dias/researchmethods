Why These Rules Exist

Every consent form, every IRB application, every awkward "may I ask you a few questions" script exists because of real harm done to real people. This history is not a formality — it is the reason the rest of this page matters.
The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972): The U.S. Public Health Service enrolled about 400 Black men with syphilis in Macon County, Alabama, to observe the disease's untreated natural course — without telling them they had it. When penicillin became a proven cure in 1945, researchers withheld it so the study could continue. It ran for 40 years and only ended after a 1972 newspaper exposed it. President Clinton formally apologized in 1997.
Tuskegee was not the only one 

Between 1946 and 1948, U.S.-supported researchers deliberately infected over a thousand people in Guatemala — including prisoners and psychiatric patients — with syphilis and other sexually transmitted infections, without consent, to test whether penicillin could prevent infection. It stayed hidden until a historian discovered it in 2010, after which President Obama issued a formal apology to Guatemala. Two governments, two apologies, six decades apart — a pattern, not an isolated failure.

These abuses led directly to the Belmont Report (1979) — the single most influential document in U.S. research ethics — and to the federal regulations that created Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) to review research before it happens, not after.

The Belmont Report's Three Principles

Every consent form and every IRB decision ultimately traces back to three ideas.

1. Respect for Persons
What it means: People are autonomous decision-makers who deserve full disclosure and a real, uncoerced choice to participate.
In practice: Consent is a conversation, not a signature. A "no" never needs justification — and researchers must actively watch for settings (classrooms, clinics, street outreach) where saying no feels socially harder than saying yes.
2. Beneficence
What it means: Maximize benefit, minimize harm — and the risks disclosed must be honest and complete, not just physical.
In practice: Most health research carries little physical risk but real psychosocial risk — if a person's HIV status, substance use, or mental health diagnosis became public, the damage could be significant even though no needle was ever involved.
3. Justice
What it means: The people who bear a study's risks should be among those who can reasonably benefit from its findings.
In practice: From 2005–2011, HIV-prevention drug trials were conducted in Botswana — one of the world's poorer nations. The resulting drug now costs roughly $1,500 a month. The population that made the discovery possible could not realistically afford what it helped create — a textbook violation of justice, even though the study itself was scientifically sound.
A related idea worth knowing: clinical equipoise. It's considered ethical to randomize participants to different treatments only when there is genuine, honest uncertainty within the expert medical community about which treatment is actually better. Once real evidence tips the balance, continuing to randomize people to the arm now known to be worse stops being research — it becomes withholding a better treatment, which is exactly what beneficence forbids. Notice the tension: protecting minors (respect for autonomy) can conflict with including them in research that could genuinely help them (justice). Researchers can request a waiver of parental consent when requiring it would itself put a young participant at risk — but only the IRB decides whether that argument holds.

Populations That Get Extra Protection

Because history shows these groups have been exploited before, federal regulations require IRBs to apply extra scrutiny whenever research involves them.

👶 Children & minors — cannot legally consent, can only assent; parental permission is normally required
⚖️ Prisoners — incarceration itself can make "voluntary" participation coercive
🪥 Pregnant women, fetuses & neonates — research may need permission from both parents
🧠 People with diminished decision-making capacity — require additional safeguards around consent

These protections aren't just good practice — they're written into federal law. 45 CFR 46 (Title 45, Part 46 of the Code of Federal Regulations, also called the Common Rule) is what actually requires the IRB review covered next, and it's organized into subparts that map almost exactly onto the groups above.

Not Every Study Gets the Same Review

Once you know your project counts as research (it is designed to create generalizable knowledge, not just answer a one-off local question), an IRB sorts it into one of three review tiers.

Exempt
Least demanding. Fits one of six defined low-risk categories — e.g., anonymous surveys, or research using existing public data.
Expedited
Moderately demanding. No more than minimal risk. One reviewer decides, instead of the full board.
Full Committee Review
Most demanding. Involves greater-than-minimal risk or a vulnerable population. A full committee of 12 or more members must review it together.
You can guess which tier fits your study — but the local IRB always makes the final call. When in doubt, ask before you start collecting data, not after.

Try it yourself

Read each short scenario, decide which tier you would guess, then reveal the reasoning.

Scenario A: An anonymous, 5-minute online survey about study habits, open to any adult 

Likely: Exempt. Anonymous survey research on non-sensitive topics with adult participants fits squarely into one of the six exempt categories — there is no way to link responses back to a person.

Scenario B: Drawing a small blood sample from healthy adult volunteers, twice, two weeks apart 

Likely: Expedited. It is an invasive procedure, so it cannot be exempt — but the amount and frequency described falls within the minimal-risk thresholds that qualify for expedited review.

Scenario C: Testing a new HIV-prevention counseling program with homeless youth under 18 

Likely: Full Committee Review. Minors are a protected population, homelessness adds psychosocial risk and consent complications, and the topic carries real potential for harm if confidentiality is ever broken — all three push this to the most demanding tier.
