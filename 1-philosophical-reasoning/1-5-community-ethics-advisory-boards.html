Passing IRB Review Is Not the Same as Earning Trust

An IRB can tell you a study is legally and procedurally allowed. It cannot always tell you whether a community will find it respectful, useful, or worth their time. That gap is exactly what this page covers.

The Belmont principles from 1.4 protect individuals. But health research increasingly happens with whole communities — and communities have collective histories, priorities, and forms of trust (or distrust) that an individual consent form was never designed to capture.

Community Advisory Boards (CABs)

What a CAB Is
A standing group of community members — often sharing the identity, illness experience, culture, or neighborhood the research focuses on — who formally advise a research team throughout a project, not just at the consent stage.
What a CAB Actually Does
Reviews study materials for language and framing that could unintentionally stigmatize or alienate participants.
Flags recruitment approaches that look fine on paper but would feel intrusive or unsafe in practice.
Advises on how findings should be shared back with the community — not just published in a journal nobody in the community will ever read.
Surfaces community priorities the research team would not have thought to ask about.
This is the infrastructure behind community-based participatory research (CBPR) — an approach where community members are treated as partners across the entire study, from designing the research question to deciding how results get used, rather than simply enrolling as participants at the end.

Two Different Questions, Asked Together

An IRB and a CAB are not competitors — they check different things, and a study benefits from both.

An IRB Asks
Does this meet the federal definition of minimal risk?
Is the consent form complete, accurate, and legally sufficient?
Are vulnerable populations given the required extra protections?
A CAB Asks
Does this respect how our community actually lives, not just how the protocol assumes we live?
Will this build trust with future participants, or spend it?
Who from our community benefits when this study is over?

The APHA Public Health Code of Ethics

The American Public Health Association's Code of Ethics (revised 2019) names six core values that apply whether or not a project is formally classified as "research." Public health work that never touches an IRB is still bound by these.

Professionalism & Trust
Public trust is earned through decisions grounded in evidence, competence, and honesty about conflicting interests — not authority alone.
Health & Safety
Practitioners have a direct obligation to prevent and reduce harm, not just document it after the fact.
Health Justice & Equity
Burdens and benefits should be distributed fairly — and practitioners should address the structural conditions that create inequity, not just its symptoms.
Interdependence & Solidarity
Individual, community, and environmental health are linked — a decision that helps one group at another's expense is rarely a genuine public-health win.
Human Rights & Civil Liberties
Even well-intentioned public-health action must respect personal autonomy, privacy, and self-determination.
Inclusivity & Engagement
Decisions that affect a community should be made with transparent, accountable input from that community — the same spirit that drives CABs.
Source: American Public Health Association, Public Health Code of Ethics (2019 revision). Notice how directly "Inclusivity & Engagement" restates the reason CABs exist — this is not a coincidence; both grew out of the same recognition that ethical practice requires the people affected to have a real voice.

Which Value Is Doing the Work?

Read each short situation, decide which APHA value or values it mainly tests, then reveal one reasonable answer. (More than one value can apply — that is normal.)

A city health department wants to publish neighborhood-level overdose data to guide funding — but the smallest neighborhoods have so few residents that individuals could be identified. 

Mainly: Human Rights & Civil Liberties (privacy), balanced against Health Justice & Equity (the data could direct real resources to the neighborhoods that need them most). Neither value simply overrides the other — this is exactly the kind of tension a CAB is well suited to help think through.

A university team wants to study a rare genetic condition concentrated in one small, tightly-knit community, and plans to name the community in their published paper. 

Mainly: Inclusivity & Engagement (did the community get a say in how it would be identified?) and Interdependence & Solidarity (findings that stigmatize the community would harm it long after the study ends). A CAB would likely flag the naming decision before publication, not after.
