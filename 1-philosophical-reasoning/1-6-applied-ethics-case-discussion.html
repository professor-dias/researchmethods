Now Put It Together

Real studies rarely present one clean ethical issue at a time. Read each short case, decide for yourself which principle (or principles) from 1.4 and 1.5 are actually in tension, then reveal one reasonable way to think it through. There is often more than one defensible answer — the goal is noticing the tension in the first place.

Case A · The Easiest Sample in the Building

A professor wants to survey students about alcohol use. The fastest option: hand out the survey during her own 200-person lecture and collect it at the end of class.
What would you flag? 

Respect for persons. A classroom is a social environment with a real power gradient — the professor grades these students. Even with a verbal "participation is optional," students may not feel free to decline in front of peers and an authority figure, and collecting papers in the room (rather than a dropbox) makes non-participation visible. A better design separates the invitation from the professor entirely, or moves data collection outside class time.

Case B · A Community With a Name

Researchers studying a rare inherited condition find it concentrated in one small rural community. Their draft paper names the town, since it makes the finding easier for other scientists to locate and verify.
What would you flag? 

Inclusivity & engagement, and interdependence & solidarity. Scientific convenience is not the only cost-benefit calculation that matters. Naming the town could invite unwanted attention, stigma, or even affect things like local property values and insurance perceptions — harms that fall on people who never individually agreed to be identified. A community advisory board would likely have caught this before submission, not after a reviewer or the community itself raised it.

Case C · The Trial That Can't Afford Its Own Results

A five-year clinical trial in a low-income region successfully proves a new preventive drug works. The drug is approved and hits the market at $1,200 a month — far beyond what most participants in the original trial could ever pay.
What would you flag? 

Justice. The Belmont Report requires that the population bearing a study's risk be a reasonable beneficiary of its findings. A trial can be methodologically flawless and still violate this principle if there is no realistic plan — a subsidized access program, a post-trial provision agreement, anything — for the study population to actually benefit from what their participation helped prove.

Case D · The Consent Form That Could Cause the Harm It's Meant to Prevent

A study on contraceptive use among sexually active teens legally requires parental consent. But contacting parents risks "outing" teens who have not told their families they are sexually active — in a population where that disclosure could lead to real harm at home.
What would you flag? 

Justice versus respect for persons — a genuine, well-documented tension. Excluding these teens denies them a study that could help people exactly like them (a justice problem). Requiring standard parental consent could put an individual teen at risk (an autonomy and safety problem). This is precisely the situation a waiver of parental consent exists for — but the IRB, not the researcher alone, has to be convinced the waiver is justified.
