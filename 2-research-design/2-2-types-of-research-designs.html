Observational Designs

Three designs cover most observational health research — and the only real difference between them is when you're looking.

Cross-SectionalCase-ControlCohort
Snapshot or timeline?One single point in timeLooks backward from outcome to exposureFollows a group forward through time
Starting pointA sample of the populationPeople who already have the outcome ("cases") vs. those who don't ("controls")People who share an exposure (or don't), before any outcome exists yet
Best forPrevalence, quick surveillance, generating hypothesesRare diseases — you don't have to wait for rare events to happenEstablishing that exposure came before outcome

Notice the trade-off: cross-sectional is the "Ferrari" from 2.1 — fast, but it can only ever show you a correlation. Cohort studies are the "semitruck" — slow and resource-heavy, but strong enough to establish that the exposure happened first, which is a real step toward a causal claim.
A quick worked example of each 

Cross-sectional: Survey 1,000 adults this month about current exercise habits and current blood pressure. You get a snapshot — you can say the two are related, but not which caused which.

Case-control: Identify 200 people with a rare cancer and 200 similar people without it, then look backward at both groups' exposure histories to find what differs.

Cohort: Enroll 5,000 smokers and 5,000 non-smokers who are all currently disease-free, then follow both groups for ten years to see who develops the disease.

Prospective vs. Retrospective

This is a second, separate timing question that applies mainly to cohort studies — and it's easy to confuse with the observational-design choice above.

Prospective
You enroll people today and follow them into the future, collecting new data as it happens.
Slower, but the data collected is exactly what you need — no gaps.
Retrospective
You reconstruct a cohort from existing records — medical charts, registries — that already span the relevant time period.
Much faster and cheaper, but you're stuck with whatever the records happened to capture.
Case-control designs are always retrospective by nature — you already know who has the outcome, and you're looking backward at their exposure history. That's exactly why case-control studies carry a real risk of recall bias: people don't always remember their own past exposures accurately.
A few more observational variants you'll encounter 

Case report / case series: A detailed write-up of one patient (or a handful) with an unusual presentation. Not generalizable on its own — but historically how entirely new conditions get first noticed. This is exactly how the AIDS epidemic was first flagged in the early 1980s.

Ecological: The unit of analysis is a group (a country, a school, a city), not an individual — e.g., comparing soda tax rates to obesity rates across states. Fast and cheap, but risks the ecological fallacy: a pattern true for groups isn't guaranteed to be true for the individuals inside them.

Successive independent samples: A repeated cross-sectional design — a fresh, independently drawn sample from the same population at several points in time (e.g., a national survey repeated every year). Good for tracking trends; still can't establish individual-level cause and effect.

Case-crossover: Cases serve as their own controls, comparing a person's exposure right before an event to their own exposure at other times. Useful for brief, transient exposures (e.g., using a cell phone right before a car crash).

Experimental & Quasi-Experimental Designs

Once you've decided to intervene, one more decision separates the two families of experimental design: does a coin flip decide who gets the intervention?

True Experimental
Participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control — this is what lets you rule out "the groups were just different to begin with."
Posttest-only control group — simplest version, no pretest.
Pretest-posttest control group — adds a baseline measure, so you can confirm the groups started out equivalent.
Multi-arm RCT — more than one treatment condition compared against a shared control.
Matched pairs — each participant (or site) is paired with a similar partner before randomizing each pair.
Within-subjects / crossover — the same people experience every condition, in randomized order, acting as their own control.
Quasi-Experimental
Groups are compared, but assignment is not random — often because randomizing isn't ethical or practical.
Nonequivalent control group design — e.g., comparing two existing schools, one that adopted a new policy and one that didn't. Susceptible to differential selection (2.3), since the groups may have differed before the study began.
Interrupted time series — many measurements taken before and after an intervention on the same group, watching for a break in the trend line. Considered the strongest quasi-experimental design.
Researchers sometimes write a design out in shorthand: O1 X O2 means "observe, then treat, then observe again" for one group. Adding a second, untreated row (O2 alone, no X) turns a weak "pre-experimental" one-group design into a real comparison.

Implementing an Observational Study in Practice

This is Step 7 from 2.1 — "Is Autopilot a Good Idea?" — applied specifically to observational work. A good design on paper still has to survive contact with real people.

Gaining access: Wherever you recruit — clinics, schools, online panels — you need a realistic plan for reaching your defined population, not just the people who are easiest to find.
Recruiting systematically: Use the same script, the same criteria, and the same pitch for every potential participant — inconsistent recruiting quietly introduces bias before data collection even starts.
Minimizing attrition: People drop out of longer studies. Plan for it — and check whether the people who leave look different from the people who stay, since that difference itself can bias your results.
Standardizing measurement: The same question, asked the same way, by every interviewer, every time — or your "data" partly measures your data collectors instead of your participants.
