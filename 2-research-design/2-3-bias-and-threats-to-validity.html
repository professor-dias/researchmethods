The Study Was Well Designed. The Answer Was Still Wrong.

A design from 2.2 tells you what shape a study has — cross-sectional, cohort, RCT. It doesn't guarantee the study got the right answer. Every design has its own specific weak points, and this page is the field guide to them.

You can pick the perfect design, recruit real people, run the whole study exactly on paper — and still land on a conclusion that's quietly wrong. Something other than your treatment or exposure explains the result.

That "something else" almost always falls into one of two buckets: bias (a systematic pull in one direction) or a threat to internal validity (a specific, well-documented way that pull sneaks in). This page is about learning to spot both.

It's worth slowing down for — this is one of the ideas in this course that's easiest to forget and most important to keep.

A bigger sample fixes the top-right archer — more arrows tighten the scatter around the same, correct center. No sample size on earth fixes the bottom-left archer. Every additional arrow just confirms, more confidently, that you're aiming at the wrong spot. That's what bias is: a systematic pull toward the wrong answer that more data cannot undo. The nine threats below are the specific, well-documented ways that pull gets introduced into a study.

Internal vs. External Validity

Bias threatens one specific kind of validity. It helps to separate it from a second, easily-confused kind.

Internal Validity
"Within this study, did the treatment or exposure actually cause the outcome?"
Threatened by confounders and the nine threats on this page — anything that offers a rival explanation for your result.
Strongest in tightly controlled designs: true experiments > quasi-experiments > observational.
External Validity
"Outside this study, does the result hold up — for other people, places, and times?"
Threatened by narrow samples, artificial settings, and unusually motivated participants.
Often traded off against internal validity: the tighter the control, the less the setting resembles real life.
Example: a lab RCT recruits only healthy psychology undergraduates, runs them through a stress-reduction app in a quiet, controlled room for one week, and finds a clear effect. Internal validity is strong — random assignment, a controlled setting, a real effect within this sample. External validity is a separate, open question — will the same effect show up in an exhausted night-shift nurse using the app on a commute, over a full semester? This study alone can't say. Neither kind of validity is "better" — they're just different questions, and a strong study is honest about which one it answers.

The Nine Threats to Internal Validity

These are the specific, named ways a rival explanation can sneak into a study and get mistaken for a real effect. You don't need to memorize definitions word-for-word — you need to recognize the pattern when you see it, which is exactly what the case clinic below is for.

🔦
Hawthorne Effect
People behave differently simply because they know they're being watched or are part of a study — the observation itself changes the outcome.
Watch for: any study where participants know their status
💧
Diffusion / Contamination
The control group picks up some part of the treatment anyway, usually through contact with treated participants — shrinking the real gap between groups.
Watch for: groups that share a building, staff, or social network
📰
History
Some outside event happens during the study — unrelated to your treatment — and it affects everyone's outcome anyway.
Watch for: long-running studies, especially interrupted time series
🌱
Maturation
Participants change naturally over time — aging, healing, learning — and that natural change gets mistaken for a treatment effect.
Watch for: children, adolescents, or any condition that changes on its own
🔁
Testing Effect
Simply taking a pretest changes posttest performance — practice, familiarity, remembered answers — apart from any real change.
Watch for: the same instrument used twice, pretest & posttest
📏
Instrumentation
The measurement tool, criteria, or observer changes partway through the study, so a "change" in the data is partly just a change in how it was measured.
Watch for: long studies, human raters, a swapped or "improved" survey
↩️
Statistical Regression
Participants selected because they scored unusually high or low will naturally drift back toward average next time — treatment or not.
Watch for: recruitment based on an extreme score
⚖️
Differential Selection
The comparison groups were already different before the study started, so an "effect" may just be the pre-existing difference.
Watch for: any design without random assignment (2.2's nonequivalent control group)
🚪
Differential Attrition
Dropout isn't random — a specific kind of person leaves one group more than the other — so who's left no longer represents who you started with.
Watch for: long or demanding studies, especially the treatment arm
