Go Find the Pixels That Already Exist

In 2.1, you watched a full picture emerge from a hundred small, independent pixels. A literature review is how you deliberately go look at the pixels other researchers have already published — before you spend a year producing one of your own.

Skipping this step has a real cost: you risk re-answering a question that's already answered, missing a measurement tool that already exists, or worse — falling for the hasty generalization from 1.1 by treating one small study as if it settled something big.

Step 1: Scope It Before You Search

Recall Step 2 from the 2.1 roadmap: narrow beats broad. The same rule applies here — a vague search returns thousands of barely-relevant results, while a precise one returns a manageable, genuinely useful set.

Write your question down first. "What affects diabetes?" is unsearchable. "Does perceived social support predict medication adherence in adults with type 2 diabetes?" is.
Decide your inclusion/exclusion criteria in advance. Population, time frame, language, study type — decide before you search, not after you've already seen the results (deciding afterward invites you to cherry-pick).

Step 2: Search Strategically

A good search is systematic, not a single Google query.

Where to Search
PubMed / MEDLINE — the standard for biomedical & health literature
PsycINFO — behavioral & psychological research
CINAHL — nursing & allied health
Google Scholar — broad coverage, useful for cross-checking
How to Search
Combine keywords with AND (narrows), OR (broadens synonyms), NOT (excludes) — e.g., (adolescent OR teen) AND vaping AND (cessation OR quitting)
Many researchers focus on the last 5 years for currency — but always include the older, foundational studies a topic is actually built on.

Step 3: Screen It Down, Then Snowball

A real search casts a wide net and then narrows deliberately. Here's what that narrowing typically looks like:

Found in databases
842
After removing duplicates
610
After title/abstract screening
96
Included after full-text review
23

This is the logic behind PRISMA, the reporting standard used for formal systematic reviews — you don't need a full systematic review for a class paper, but tracking your own numbers this way keeps your process honest and reproducible.

Then snowball: chase citations in both directions 

Backward snowballing: Once you find one strong, relevant paper, check its reference list — it just did a chunk of your literature search for you.

Forward snowballing: Use "cited by" (Google Scholar, PubMed) to see who has cited that paper since it was published — this is often how you find the newest, most relevant work.

Step 4: Critique What You Read

Not every published paper earns a place in your review. Before a study goes into your synthesis matrix, run it through a systematic critique — the same handful of questions, asked of every paper, so your judgment is consistent instead of based on a gut feeling about how convincing it sounded.

Step 5: Synthesize, Don't Just Summarize

The single most common mistake in a student literature review: a list of paragraphs that each say "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2021) found Y." That's a summary, not a review. A real review is organized by theme, not by paper.

Study 1Study 2Study 3
PopulationAdults, type 2 diabetesAdults, type 2 diabetesOlder adults, type 2 diabetes
DesignCross-sectionalCohortCross-sectional
Key findingSupport ↑ associated with adherence ↑Support at baseline predicted adherence 1 year laterNo significant association found

A synthesis matrix like this — studies as columns, key features as rows — lets you literally see the pattern (and the disagreement in Study 3) across studies at a glance, instead of buried in three separate paragraphs.

This table is also your best defense against the hasty-generalization fallacy from 1.1: laid out side by side, it's obvious that Study 3 disagrees with the other two — a pattern you'd likely miss if you read and summarized each study in isolation.

Step 6: Find the Gap

Every well-run literature review ends with the same question: given everything above, what still isn't known? That gap — a population nobody's studied, a relationship nobody's tested, findings that disagree with each other — is exactly the raw material 2.5 asks you to turn into a problem statement.

You now have everything you need to start building: a well-scoped question, a synthesized picture of what's already known, and a genuine gap. 2.5 is where you turn that gap into a problem statement, a purpose, a research question, and a hypothesis — one continuous worksheet.
