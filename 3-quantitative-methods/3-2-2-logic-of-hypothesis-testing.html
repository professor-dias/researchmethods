Where Does an Outcome Come From?

You just watched effect size, a confidence interval, and a p-value work inside two examples (3.2.1) — a card game and a drug trial. Now let's build the vocabulary and logic underneath them properly, so they travel with you into every real test you'll ever run.

Whatever you measure — a rate, a score, a difference between groups — has to have come from somewhere. There are only three real candidates.

Chance
Random noise. Flip a fair coin 10 times and you might get 7 heads — not because anything is "wrong," just sampling variation.
Confounders
Some other factor you didn't account for is doing the work, and it just looks like your variable is responsible (2.3).
Your Hypothesis
The specific relationship or effect you actually suspect is real — the explanation you're trying to test.
X  ←  Chance + Y1 + Y2 + Y3 + …

X is the outcome you measured. Each Y is something that might be pulling on it — a confounder, or your real hypothesis. Looking at X alone, you can't tell which Y's are doing the work.

The Outcome You Measured
One number. Three possible explanations, tangled together.
Here's the uncomfortable part: looking at the outcome alone, you cannot tell these three apart. That's not a flaw in your study — it's the actual problem hypothesis testing exists to solve.

Three Grounding Rules for Statistical Analysis

These are the ground rules. Break one and the math still runs — the software won't stop you — but the answer it hands back means far less than it looks like it does.

🌐
Sample ≠ Population
Our data can model a population, but a sample is never guaranteed to actually represent it (3.1.5).
🎲
Every Event Differs
A pattern found in one sample won't repeat exactly in the next one — some wobble from study to study is normal, not a red flag.
🔄
Correlation ≠ Causation
True for a whole body of literature, and just as true for one single result (1.1).

Why We Need Hypothesis Testing At All

We can never see the truth directly. We only ever get to see one sample.

What We Want to Know
The real state of the world — is there truly an effect, a difference, a relationship? Permanently hidden from us.
What We Actually Have
One sample, one dataset, one shot at estimating that hidden truth — standing in for a "no-effect world" we can never directly observe either.
So we do the next best thing: we build a precise model of what a no-effect world would look like, then check how well our one sample actually fits that model. That comparison is hypothesis testing.

The Null and the Alternative

Null hypothesis (H0): Nothing is going on beyond the variation we'd expect from sampling alone — no difference, no relationship, no effect.
Alternative hypothesis (H1): Something real is going on — a genuine difference, relationship, or effect exists.

The null is always the starting assumption. We don't try to prove it's true — we try to see whether our data makes it hard to believe.

Common mix-up worth naming directly: the null does not say "chance caused this." It says nothing more than that whatever we observed is within the range ordinary sampling variation would produce on its own.

Test Statistic: Signal Over Noise

A test statistic answers one question: how big is what we found, compared to the ordinary wobble we'd expect just from sampling?

Test statistic  =  the difference we observed ÷ the variation we'd expect from sampling alone
Clear Signal, Low Noise
A voice you can hear clearly over the static — large test statistic.
Faint Signal, Buried in Noise
The same size signal, but drowned in noisy variation — small test statistic, even though something real might still be there.
Same underlying idea, more or less noise. A bigger test statistic means the signal is easier to pick out from the static — which is exactly what makes a result more likely to look "significant."

P-value: How Surprising Is This Result?

Picture the no-effect world from a moment ago. The p-value asks: in that world, how often would we see a result at least this extreme?

The shaded area is the no-effect world's tail — how often it hands out a result at least this far out. A small shaded area means our result would be rare if nothing were really going on.

HHHHH HHHHH

Easy way to remember it: a fair coin landing heads 10 times in a row would make you suspicious it isn't fair. The p-value measures exactly that kind of suspicion, assuming the "fair coin" baseline is true.

Alpha and the Decision

Alpha (α) is the cutoff we choose before we look at the data — commonly .05. If the p-value comes in below alpha, we reject H0.

Alpha is a convention, not a law of nature — .05 is simply the threshold this line of work has agreed on. Whatever we choose, we can never reach 100% certainty. Alpha is the size of the false-alarm risk we've agreed to accept going in.

Effect Size: How Big Is It?

"Significant" means unlikely under the null. It does not mean big or important. Sample size can make either one show up without the other.

n = 12 per group. A real 30-point gap can still miss statistical significance — the sample was just too small and noisy to detect it reliably.
Effect size is what tells you the two apart: not just "was this unlikely to be chance," but "how large is the gap we're even talking about?"

Confidence Intervals: How Precise Is Our Estimate?

One sample gives one estimate. A different sample would give a slightly different one. A confidence interval shows the range of plausible values our estimate could have landed in.

Twenty intervals, each from a different hypothetical sample of the same population. Most cross the true value (dashed line) — a couple, by chance, miss it entirely.

The correct reading of "95% confidence": if we repeated the study many times and built an interval from each sample, about 95% of those intervals would capture the true value. It is not "a 95% probability the truth is in this one interval," and it never means "95% of the time Y causes X."

Model Fit / Prediction: How Well Does It Actually Work?

Outcomes vary from person to person. R² tells you what share of that variation your model actually accounts for.

Explained by the model
38%
Unexplained (error)
62%
An R² of .38 means 38% of the variation in the outcome lines up with your predictors — the remaining 62% is down to factors the model doesn't capture. A high R² still does not mean causation, and it does not mean the model is correctly specified.
Model fit asks a different question than significance: "Okay, we built a model — how well does it actually represent or predict the outcome?"

That distinction matters: a model can contain statistically significant predictors and still not perform particularly well. Which specific number answers this depends on what kind of model you're using.

Statistics Cannot Separate Causes on Its Own

A tiny p-value can sit directly on top of a confounder. The test only tells you the pattern is hard to explain by chance — it says nothing about why the pattern exists.

X and Y look connected (dashed line) — but a hidden confounder C is quietly driving both. Statistics alone can't tell this diagram apart from a real X→Y effect.

Ruling this out is study design's job, not statistics' job: randomization, adjustment for known confounders (2.2, 2.3), and plain subject-matter knowledge about what else could be driving both variables.

Three Takeaways

1. We never reach 100% certainty. Every conclusion in this course is reasoning under uncertainty, not proof.
2. A test tells you whether chance alone is a poor fit for your data. It does not tell you why the pattern exists.
3. Report the whole set, not just one number. P-value, effect size, confidence interval, and R² where it applies — together, not in isolation.
