Step 1: Identify a Gap & Problem Statement

Every analysis starts before you touch data — with a gap in what's currently known, or a real problem worth solving. Name it in a sentence or two: what's missing, why it matters, and who it affects.

This is the "so what?" test. If you can't state the gap in plain language, you're not ready to write a research question yet. The full walkthrough of finding and framing a gap lives in 2.4.

Step 2: Research Question

Before you touch a single variable, decide which of four things you're actually trying to do. Every remaining step depends on this one answer.

Describe
"What does this population look like?" — one group, no comparison, no claim.
Compare
"Do groups differ?" — two or more groups, one shared outcome.
Relate
"Are two variables associated?" — no groups, just a relationship.
Predict
"Can I forecast one variable from others?" — one or more predictors, one target.
This is the same four-way split from 3.2.2, applied for real this time — the full method-by-method map lives in 3.2.4. Write your answer down — you'll name it again explicitly in Step 6's model.

Write down every variable your question needs, in two tables — one for your outcome, one for every exposure you're testing against it. Filling these in now saves real confusion during cleaning and analysis later.

Step 6: Modeling & Assumption Checking

Now say out loud, in one line, what you're actually testing. Using the variables from Step 3:

Outcome  ←  Exposure 1 + Exposure 2 + Exposure 3

That's the model. Everything from here on — assumptions, the test itself, the results — is about that one specific, written-down statement.

Before you can trust what that model tells you, confirm the conditions it depends on.

👥
Independence
One observation doesn't influence another. This is a design question (3.1.5), not something a formula checks for you.
📇
Normality
The outcome (or a model's residuals) roughly follows a normal, bell-shaped distribution.
Check: histogram, Q–Q plot, or a Shapiro–Wilk test
⚖️
Equal Variance
The groups you're comparing spread out by roughly the same amount, not wildly different amounts.
Check: a Levene's test
📋
Adequate Sample
Enough data for the test to behave the way its theory assumes — e.g., chi-square wants expected cell counts of 5 or more.

You don't have to memorize a checklist for every single test. One decision carries almost all of the weight:

Do normality & equal variance roughly hold?
Yes
Parametric test
t-test, ANOVA, Pearson r, linear regression
No
Non-parametric alternative
Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman, rank-based methods
A non-parametric test isn't a "weaker" backup plan — it's the correct choice when the parametric assumptions don't hold. Choosing it on purpose is good statistics, not giving up. See 3.2.10 for where each option lives.

Step 7: Choose the Test

Everything from Steps 2–6 — your question, your variables, your design, your model, your assumption check — funnels into one decision, made of three parts.

7a · Outcome Type
Continuous, Binary, Ordinal, Count, or Time-to-event — named in Step 3's table
7b · Study Design
How many groups, paired or independent, repeated over time?
7c · Choose the Test
Outcome type + design + Step 6's assumption check → exactly one method
Three questions, asked in order every time: what's my outcome, what's my design, and did my assumptions hold? That's exactly what the Test Selector Tool asks you next.

Step 8: Interpret & Report

A finished analysis becomes a finished study only once it's written up honestly — findings and limitations together, never findings alone.

State the finding in plain language first, then back it with the numbers — p-value, effect size, confidence interval, and R² where it applies (3.2.2).
Match your claim to your design. An observational design earns "associated with," not "caused" (2.2, 3.2.2's causation caveat).
Don't forget limitations — ever. Name the specific threats to validity that plausibly apply to your study (2.3): sampling, measurement, missing data, generalizability. Generic disclaimers don't count; name the actual ones.
