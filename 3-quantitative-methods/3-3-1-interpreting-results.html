Interpreting Results

Getting output is the easy part. Reading it correctly is where the real skill is.

Effect sizes
Report these alongside every p-value: Cohen's d, r, eta-squared, or an odds ratio. A p-value tells you whether there's an effect; an effect size tells you how big it is.
Confidence intervals
A range of plausible values for the true effect. A narrow interval means a precise estimate; a wide one means you should be cautious about trusting the exact number.
Statistical vs. practical significance
A p-value below .05 means the result is unlikely under the null hypothesis — nothing more. It does not tell you the result is important. Practical significance asks a separate question: is this effect large enough to matter in the real world?
Avoiding p-value traps
A non-significant result is not proof of "no effect" — it might just mean your study was too small to detect one. And a significant result is not proof of a large or meaningful effect — with enough data, even a trivial difference can become "significant."
