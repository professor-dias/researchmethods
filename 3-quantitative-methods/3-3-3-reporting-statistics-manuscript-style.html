The Numbers Are Right. Now Write Them Correctly.

6.2 covers where results go in a manuscript. This page covers how the numbers themselves get written once they're there — the small, precise formatting conventions that signal a careful analyst to a reviewer.

Statistical results follow a compact, standardized notation — test name, degrees of freedom, the statistic itself, and a p-value, almost always in that order. Once you know the pattern, reading (and writing) results sections gets dramatically faster.

Reading the Notation

TestReported AsExample
Independent t-testt(df) = value, p = valuet(48) = 2.31, p = .025
One-way ANOVAF(df1, df2) = value, p = valueF(2, 87) = 5.40, p = .006
Correlationr(df) = value, p = valuer(118) = .34, p < .001
Chi-squareχ²(df, N = n) = value, p = valueχ²(1, N = 214) = 6.72, p = .010
Regression coefficientb = value (or β), p = valueb = 0.42, p = .003
Degrees of freedom go in parentheses immediately after the test statistic's letter, with no space — t(48), not t (48) or t48. It's a small detail, but it's one of the fastest ways a careful reader clocks an unpolished draft.

The Small Rules That Add Up

Rounding and leading zeros 

Most statistics are reported to two decimal places. Values that can never exceed 1 — p-values, correlations, proportions — drop the leading zero: write p = .04, not p = 0.04. Values that can exceed 1 — means, standard deviations, most test statistics — keep it: M = 0.42, not M = .42.

Reporting p-values 

Report the exact value to two or three decimal places (p = .032), rather than just "p < .05" — exact values let a reader judge how close a result was to the threshold. The one common exception: very small p-values are reported as p < .001 rather than a string of leading zeros.

Non-significant results still get reported in full 

A non-significant finding is not "no result" — it's still a result, and it still needs the full notation: t(48) = 1.12, p = .268. Omitting the statistics for a null finding (or reporting only "not significant") hides information a reader needs to judge whether the study had adequate power to detect an effect at all.

Effect size belongs next to the p-value, not instead of it 

A p-value says whether an effect is unlikely to be chance; an effect size (Cohen's d, r, odds ratio, η²) says how big it actually is. Report both: "the intervention group showed significantly lower stress, t(112) = 3.05, p = .003, d = 0.58." A large sample can make a trivial effect statistically significant — the effect size is what tells a reader whether it's practically significant too.

Confidence intervals: report the interval, not just the point estimate 

Wherever possible, give the interval alongside the estimate: "the mean difference was 4.2 points, 95% CI [1.1, 7.3]." This does double duty as a plausible-range statement and an implicit significance test — a 95% CI that excludes zero (for a difference) or 1 (for a ratio) corresponds to p < .05.

A Worked Results Sentence

"Adherence scores were significantly higher in the intervention group (M = 7.8, SD = 1.4) than the control group (M = 6.5, SD = 1.6), t(112) = 4.62, p < .001, d = 0.87, 95% CI for the mean difference [0.75, 1.85]."

Notice everything a reader needs is here in one sentence: group means and spread, the test used, its degrees of freedom, the exact statistic, statistical significance, effect size, and a confidence interval — no separate paragraph required to reconstruct any of it.
