Multiple Testing

The question: If I run lots of tests, how many "discoveries" are just false alarms?

Remember the coin-flip logic from Chapter 1: even with no real effect, a 5% false-alarm rate means 1 test in 20 will look "significant" purely by luck. Run enough tests, and false alarms are guaranteed.

Flagship idea: The more you test, the more false alarms pile up
Why we do this: Researchers rarely run just one test — they often check dozens of variables at once.
The logic: Each individual test keeps its own 5% false-alarm rate. But run 20 of them on data with zero real effects, and on average one will still come back "significant," purely by chance.
What to look for: How many tests you ran in total, not just how many came back significant.
What it means: Correction methods make each individual test stricter, so the whole batch together still only has a 5% false-alarm rate.

Correction methods

MethodIn plain words
BonferroniDivide your significance threshold by the number of tests — simple, but very strict
HolmA step-by-step version of Bonferroni that's a little less strict
HochbergSimilar to Holm, worked from the opposite end of the list
Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR)Instead of guarding against any false alarm, controls the overall share of discoveries that turn out to be false
Benjamini-YekutieliA stricter version of FDR control for when tests are related to each other
ŠidákA small mathematical refinement of Bonferroni for independent tests
