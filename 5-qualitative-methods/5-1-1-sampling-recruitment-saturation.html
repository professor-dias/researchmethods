Sample Size Isn't a Power Calculation Here

In 3.1.5, sample size was a number you calculated in advance from an effect size and a power target. Qualitative work flips that: you decide who before you know exactly how many — and you stop collecting data when the data itself tells you to.

That's not a lower bar — it's a different question entirely. A survey asks "how much of the population does this represent?" A qualitative study asks "have I heard the full range of what there is to hear?" The second question can't be answered with a formula before you start. It's answered by a concept called saturation, and by a sampling strategy built to reach it on purpose.

How You Choose Participants

Random sampling is the gold standard when your goal is representativeness. It's rarely the right tool here — your goal is depth and range of experience, not a miniature version of the population.

🎯
Purposive
You deliberately select people who have the experience or characteristic your question is about — e.g., only patients who discontinued a treatment.
Best for: a clearly defined population of interest
⚫
Maximum Variation
A deliberate purposive variant: you recruit for the widest possible range of the experience — different ages, settings, severity — to see what holds true across all of them.
Best for: finding what's common across very different people
✨
Snowball
Existing participants refer you to others who share the characteristic you're studying — essential when the population is hidden or hard to reach directly.
Best for: stigmatized, rare, or hard-to-list populations
📊
Theoretical
Used in grounded theory specifically: who you recruit next is chosen based on what your analysis so far suggests you still need to understand — sampling and analysis run at the same time.
Best for: grounded theory, building an emerging theory
These aren't mutually exclusive. A common real pattern: start purposive to get your core group, add maximum variation to stress-test what you're finding, and snowball when purposive recruitment alone can't reach enough people.

So How Many People, Roughly?

You still need a planning number for your IRB application and your own scheduling — you just don't get it from a power calculation. Instead, use the norms your specific methodology has built up over decades of published work.

MethodologyTypical RangeWhy
Phenomenology6–10 participantsDeep, detailed accounts of one shared lived experience — depth over breadth
Grounded Theory20–30 participantsEnough variation to build and saturate a theory's every category
EthnographyVaries widelyDriven by time in the field, not an interview count
Case Study1 case (with multiple data sources/participants within it)Depth on one bounded case, not breadth across many
General qualitative description12–20 participantsA common working range for interview-based applied health research

Treat these as starting targets, not contracts. The real stopping rule is saturation — see below — and you write your justification in the plan, not the pretense that a formula produced it.

Saturation: Watching the New Ideas Run Out

Saturation is the point where new interviews stop producing new codes or new themes — you're hearing variations on what you've already heard, not anything genuinely new. It's the qualitative equivalent of "the confidence interval stopped shrinking": more data isn't buying you more information.

Interviews 1–5
9 new codes
Interviews 6–10
5 new codes
Interviews 11–15
2 new codes
Interviews 16–20
0 new codes
This is what a real saturation curve looks like: a steep climb early, then diminishing returns, then flat. Once you've gone a pre-specified number of interviews (commonly two to three in a row) with zero new codes, you've reached saturation — that's your stopping point, decided by the data, not by a number you picked in advance.
Writing the Justification
State your planning target and which methodology norm it's based on.
Report the actual point saturation was reached (e.g., "no new codes emerged after interview 17; two further interviews confirmed no additional codes").
If you stopped before saturation (e.g., a hard recruitment ceiling), say so directly — that's a real limitation, not something to obscure.
A Related, Sharper Idea
Information power (an alternative to saturation): the more relevant information your sample already holds — a narrow research question, highly specific participants, strong theory — the fewer participants you need.
Under this view, a small, information-rich sample can outperform a larger, more generic one.

Recruitment in Practice

This is the same "Step 7: Is Autopilot a Good Idea?" question from 2.1, applied to qualitative recruiting specifically — a strategy that's elegant on paper still has to survive contact with real people who have real schedules and real reasons to say no.

Define eligibility precisely, in writing, before you recruit anyone. "People with anxiety" is not a criterion. "Adults 18–35 with a clinician-confirmed generalized anxiety diagnosis within the past 12 months" is.
Track who declines, and why, if they tell you. A recruitment pool that systematically excludes people who are busiest, sickest, or most distrustful of research is a sample bias problem before your first interview even happens.
Budget for over-recruitment. Interviews get rescheduled, consent gets withdrawn, audio fails. Plan to approach more people than your target number.
Compensate fairly and consistently. The same incentive, offered the same way, to everyone eligible — inconsistent incentives are a quiet form of differential selection.
