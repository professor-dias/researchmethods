The Group Is the Point, Not the Individuals In It

A focus group is a moderator interviewing several people at once — typically 6 to 12 strangers (8 is a common sweet spot), deliberately homogeneous on whatever characteristic matters for your question (age, diagnosis, role, background). But the goal isn't to collect 8 individual interviews in one room. It's to capture the interaction — participants building on, correcting, and pushing back on each other in ways no one would produce answering alone.

Too few participants and there's not enough range to spark real discussion. Too many and the group becomes unmanageable, with quieter members squeezed out entirely. The right size depends on the topic's complexity and the participants' age and comfort speaking up — not a fixed rule.

What You Gain, What You Give Up

AdvantagesTrade-offs
Efficient — one session generates data from many participants at onceConfidentiality can't be fully guaranteed; you can ask participants to keep it in the room, but can't enforce it

The Moderator's Actual Job

Set the ground rules up front. Confidentiality expectations, "no wrong answers," and that you want to hear disagreement, not just consensus.
Draw out the quiet ones. A direct, low-pressure invitation ("What about you — any thoughts?") is often enough to rebalance a group one person is dominating.
Let disagreement happen. A group that agrees on everything within the first two minutes usually isn't disagreeing out loud — that's a moderator problem, not a finding.
Manage time without cutting off momentum. A moderator guide (5.1.4) with built-in time checkpoints keeps a rich tangent from eating the whole session.

Groups can run unstructured (the moderator names a broad topic and lets the conversation go) or semistructured (a guide sequences specific questions and topics) — the more sensitive or unfamiliar the topic, the more a semistructured guide helps keep the discussion useful without over-controlling it.

Deciding Whether to Stratify

Sometimes one "homogeneous" characteristic isn't enough. If a topic is sensitive across a second dimension — say, gender, or age — it's often worth running separate, stratified groups rather than one mixed group.

One Mixed Group
Simpler to recruit and schedule.
Risk: participants self-censor in front of a group they don't feel safe being candid with.
Stratified Groups
Separate sessions by, e.g., age band or gender when the topic calls for it — more sessions to run and analyze, but safer disclosure.
Example: a study of teens' exposure to sexually explicit content online ran separate groups by age and gender, specifically so participants felt comfortable answering honestly.

Recruitment & Running the Session

Focus groups are almost always recruited through non-probability sampling (3.1.5's terminology still applies) — convenience or purposive sampling from the population your question is about. That's a reasonable trade: you're not trying to generalize to a population, you're trying to understand a range of perspectives within one (5.1.1).

Record with permission, and bring a note-taker. A dedicated note-taker frees the moderator to focus entirely on the group, and gives you a backup if the recording fails.
Arrange seating so everyone can see everyone. A circle or U-shape, not rows facing the moderator — the whole point is that participants talk to each other, not just to you.
Debrief immediately after. Moderator and note-taker compare initial impressions while the session is still fresh — this becomes some of your earliest analytic memo material (5.2.1).
