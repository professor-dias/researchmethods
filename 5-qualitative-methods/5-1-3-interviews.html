Every Interview Sits Somewhere on a Structure Spectrum

The core decision in planning an interview isn't the questions themselves — it's how tightly you control the order and wording of everything you ask.

Unstructured
No fixed questions — more a guided conversation than a formal interview. Topics emerge and flow from what the participant says.
Best when you're after the meaning behind an experience and specific questions can't be written in advance without presuming the answer.
Semistructured
A set of open-ended questions, typically asked of every participant, usually in the same order — with room for follow-up probes.
The most common choice in applied health research: consistent enough to compare across participants, open enough to let people elaborate.
Notice this is a different decision from 5.2.1's inductive-vs-deductive coding choice, even though they often travel together: an unstructured interview pairs naturally with fully inductive coding, while a semistructured guide built from prior theory pairs naturally with some deductive starter codes.

Probing: Getting Past the First Answer

A probe is anything you say or do — verbal or nonverbal — to draw out more detail without steering the content of the answer. This is the skill that separates a flat, thin transcript from a rich one.

💬
Directive Probe
A direct invitation to say more: "Could you tell me a little more about that?"
🔁
Echo Probe
Paraphrase what the participant just said back to them — it signals you're listening and invites correction or elaboration.
👤
Clarification Probe
When a participant hesitates ("I don't know"), a neutral reassurance — "there's no right answer, I just want to know how you see it" — without suggesting what you're hoping to hear.
🧞
Nonverbal Probe
A nod, or simply staying silent for a beat, gives a participant room to keep going instead of the interviewer rushing to fill the pause.
Keep every probe as neutral as possible. A probe that hints at the "right" answer stops being a probe and starts being a leading question — the interview version of a threat to validity (2.3).

Building Trust Before You Ask the Hard Question

Especially with sensitive topics, the order you ask questions in matters as much as the questions themselves.

Start neutral, end sensitive. Open with something comfortable and easy to answer — it establishes a natural conversational flow before you ask anything difficult.
Be genuinely, not performatively, interested. Participants can tell the difference between an interviewer listening to respond and one listening to understand.
Consider who's doing the asking. For some sensitive topics, matching an interviewer's identity to participants' (shared gender, language, lived experience) measurably changes how much people are willing to disclose.
Trust can take more than one session to build. For deeply sensitive topics, some studies deliberately build rapport in an early conversation before the "real" interview happens.

A Working Checklist for the Session Itself

Greet the participant appropriately, explain the purpose of the interview, and obtain informed consent before starting.
Explain how the session will be recorded, and get explicit permission to record.
Arrange seating that feels comfortable and conversational, not like an interrogation.
Look at the participant, not your guide — active listening matters more than following the script perfectly.
Use the participant's own words and phrasing when you follow up — it shows you're tracking their meaning, not imposing yours.
Stay neutral: no visible judgment or opinion about what the participant shares.
Follow the natural flow of the conversation, but keep track of which guide topics still need covering.
Thank the participant at the end, and leave room for them to ask you anything.
