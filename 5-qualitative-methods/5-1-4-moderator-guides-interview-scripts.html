The Guide Is What Makes Your Interviews Comparable

A guide is the written script — questions, built-in probes, rough timing — that keeps a semistructured interview or focus group consistent across every participant or session. Without one, you're not really running the same study twelve times; you're running twelve different, uncomparable conversations.

Interview Guide
Written for one-on-one use. Every question is aimed at a single participant's individual experience.
Moderator Guide
Written for focus groups. Includes everything an interview guide has, plus explicit instructions for managing group dynamics — how to draw out quiet members, when to redirect a dominant one, how to handle disagreement (5.1.2).

The Funnel: Broad to Specific

Most well-built guides follow the same underlying shape, regardless of topic — wide and easy at the start, narrowing toward the specific and sensitive as trust builds (5.1.3).

Opening: Easy, low-stakes, sets a conversational tone. Not analyzed for content — it's there to build comfort.
Broad orienting questions: Get the participant talking generally about the topic area before you narrow in on your actual research question.
Core questions: The heart of the guide, each tied directly to what your research question needs to know — with probes written in underneath, so you don't have to invent them on the spot.
Closing: A catch-all ("Is there anything important we haven't talked about?") plus space for the participant to ask you questions.

A Worked Example

Semistructured interview guide: barriers to telehealth adoption among older adults 

Opening: "To start, can you tell me a bit about how you usually get in touch with your doctor's office?"

Broad orienting: "What have your experiences been like with video or phone appointments, if you've tried them?" Probe: "What was that experience like for you?"

Core question 1: "What, if anything, makes a video appointment feel harder than an in-person one?" Probe (echo): "You mentioned the screen freezing — can you tell me more about what that was like?"

Core question 2: "Who, if anyone, helps you get set up for a video visit?" Probe: "What happens on days when that person isn't available?"

Core question 3: "How do you decide whether to ask for a video visit or an in-person one?" Probe (clarification): "There's no right answer here — I'm just curious how you think about it."

Closing: "Is there anything about getting care this way that we haven't talked about that you think is important?"

Notice every probe is written directly under the question it belongs to. That's deliberate — if you have to improvise a good probe live, under time pressure, you'll get a weaker one than if you'd planned it in advance.

Pilot the Guide Before It's Your Real Data

A guide that reads well on paper can still fail in the room — a question that's ambiguous, one that accidentally leads, or a sequence that feels jarring. Run it with one or two people who resemble your real participants before your first real session.

Piloting isn't optional polish — it's a reportable methods step. "The interview guide was piloted with two participants and refined for clarity before use" is exactly the kind of line the 5.3.2 COREQ checklist is looking for under study design.
