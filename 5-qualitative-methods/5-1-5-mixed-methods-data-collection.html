Combined on Purpose, Not Bolted Together

Running a survey and also doing a few interviews isn't automatically "mixed methods." Mixed-methods research combines qualitative and quantitative data deliberately, to answer one connected question — the quantitative data answers "how much/how many," and the qualitative data answers "why," and the design specifies exactly how the two are meant to inform each other.

This is a direct application of the pragmatic paradigm from 5.2.1: the research question drives the method, not loyalty to one worldview or the other.

Three Common Designs

ConvergentExplanatory SequentialExploratory Sequential
OrderQual and quant collected at the same timeQuant first, then qualQual first, then quant
LogicCollect both, then merge and compare where they agree or divergeUse qual data to explain a surprising or unclear quantitative resultUse qual data to build or refine something (a theory, a scale) that quant then tests at scale
ExampleA survey on medication adherence run alongside interviews on the same topic, then comparedA survey finds unexpectedly low adherence in one subgroup → interviews with that subgroup explore whyInterviews identify barriers to adherence → those barriers become items on a new survey scale, tested in a larger sample
Notice the direction of travel matters. Explanatory sequential starts with a quantitative puzzle and uses qualitative work to explain it; exploratory sequential starts with qualitative groundwork and uses it to build something quantitative. Naming which one you're doing, and why, belongs in your methods section — it's not an implementation detail, it's part of the design.

Observation: A Method Often Combined With Interviews

Interviews and focus groups tell you what people say. Observation — watching and recording behavior in the setting where it naturally happens — tells you what people actually do, which doesn't always match. Combining the two (a form of method triangulation, 5.2.5) is one of the most common mixed-qualitative pairings in health research.

The key design decision is how involved the researcher becomes in the setting — a spectrum from fully embedded to fully detached, each with a different trade-off between insider understanding and objectivity.

👤
Complete Participant
Researcher role is covert — people in the setting don't know they're being studied. Deep insider access, but real ethical and objectivity concerns.
👁️
Participant as Observer
Researcher role is known, and they're genuinely integrated in the setting's activities. Still rich access, fewer ethical concerns than fully covert.
📋
Observer as Participant
Researcher role is known, but involvement is brief and periodic rather than embedded. More objectivity, less insider depth.
🎥
Complete Observer
No integration into the setting at all — purely watching (in person or via recording). Most objective, least able to interpret from an insider's perspective.
This is also where the Hawthorne effect (2.3) becomes a design problem, not just a threat to name: the more overtly you observe, the more your presence itself may change what you're trying to observe.

Field Notes: Observation's Version of a Transcript

Write a condensed account in the moment (or as close to it as your role allows), then expand it with full detail as soon afterward as possible — waiting even a day loses detail fast.
Separate description from interpretation. Record what happened, and separately, what you think it means — wide margins or a two-column format keep the two from blurring together.
Keep a fieldwork journal alongside your notes. Your own reactions and evolving hunches are data too (5.2.5's reflexivity) — and this journal is often where your earliest analytic memos come from.
Record the basics every time: date, site, time, and topic, at minimum — treat it with the same discipline as a transcript header.
