Before You Analyze Anything, Name Your Lens

1.2 introduced the idea that researchers reason from different paradigms — frameworks for what counts as knowledge at all. In quantitative work that choice mostly stays in the background. In qualitative work, it shapes your actual analysis, so you name it up front.

Two researchers can interview the same ten people and write genuinely different, equally rigorous papers — because they were listening through a different lens. Naming your paradigm isn't decoration for the methods section; it's the decision that determines what you're even looking for in the data.

🧠
Constructivist
Reality is built through people's lived experience and meaning-making. You're after each participant's own understanding, not one objective truth underneath it.
Common fit: phenomenology, narrative inquiry
💬
Interpretivist
Knowledge is produced through interpretation — yours and your participants' together. Meaning is socially and contextually situated, not fixed.
Common fit: thematic analysis, ethnography
⚖️
Critical
Research is never neutral — it's entangled with power, inequity, and social structure. The goal often includes surfacing whose voice has been excluded, not just describing experience.
Common fit: critical discourse analysis, participatory approaches
🛠️
Pragmatic
The research question drives the method, not allegiance to one worldview. Comfortable mixing qualitative and quantitative when the problem calls for it.
Common fit: mixed-methods designs
There's no "correct" paradigm — only a paradigm that's honest about what you're actually trying to find out. A study exploring how patients personally make sense of a diagnosis is a poor fit for a critical lens; a study of who gets excluded from a clinic's care pathway is a poor fit for a purely constructivist one.

Choosing Your Analytic Approach

Your paradigm narrows the field; your methodology is the specific, named approach that tells you exactly how to move from raw transcripts to a finished analysis. Four show up constantly in health research.

Notice these aren't ranked by rigor — they're different tools for different questions, exactly like 2.2's research designs. Naming the wrong one doesn't make your analysis wrong, but it does make your methods section internally inconsistent, and a careful reviewer will notice.

Immersing Yourself Before You Code Anything

The single most common mistake in a first qualitative project: opening a transcript and starting to code line one before you've read anything else. Immersion comes first, and it's not optional.

Read every transcript start to finish, at least once, before coding anything. You need the whole shape of what someone said before you start labeling pieces of it.
Write memos as you go. Short notes to yourself — a striking phrase, a contradiction, a hunch about a connection between two participants — captured in the moment, before you forget why it struck you.
Note your own reactions, not just the content. If something surprised you or made you uncomfortable, that's data about your own position (5.2.5 goes deeper on this) — write it down rather than letting it quietly steer your coding unexamined.
Re-read after transcription, not just after the interview. Transcripts surface things that live conversation lets you miss — pauses, repeated phrases, half-finished sentences.
