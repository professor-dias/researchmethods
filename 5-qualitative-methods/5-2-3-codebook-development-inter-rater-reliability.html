How Many People Are Doing the Coding?

This is one of the first things a careful reader checks in your methods section — because it tells them how much to trust that your codes reflect the data, and not just one person's read of it.

Single Coder
Fastest and most consistent internally — but one person's blind spots become the whole analysis's blind spots, with no built-in check.
Common in student projects and pilot work; a real limitation to name explicitly, not hide.
Dual Coder
Two coders independently code the same transcripts, then compare and reconcile. The standard expectation for a publishable qualitative study.
Lets you report a real inter-rater reliability statistic (below) — something a single coder can never produce.
Coding Team
Several coders, often with defined roles — a lead coder who maintains the codebook, and team members who apply it and flag ambiguous cases back to the lead.
Needed at scale (large datasets, multi-site studies), but adds a real coordination cost — every added coder is another perspective to reconcile.

What a Codebook Actually Contains

A codebook isn't just a list of code names — it's the shared instrument that makes two different people apply a code the same way. Every entry needs four things.

CodeDefinitionInclusion / ExclusionExample
feeling-unheardParticipant describes feeling that a provider did not listen to, acknowledge, or act on their concerns.Include: explicit or implied dismissal by a provider. Exclude: general dissatisfaction with wait times or logistics."I told her twice and she just moved on to the next question."
self-managementParticipant describes independently managing a symptom or condition without clinical guidance.Include: deliberate self-directed action. Exclude: passive coping (just enduring symptoms)."I just started adjusting the dose myself based on how I felt."
The inclusion/exclusion column is what separates a real codebook from a glossary. Without a stated boundary, two careful coders will still disagree on edge cases — the boundary is what makes disagreement resolvable instead of endless.

Reaching Consensus — and Handling Disagreement

Disagreement between coders isn't a failure of the method. It's expected, and how you handle it is itself part of your rigor.

Code independently
→
Compare code-by-code
→
Discuss every disagreement
→
Revise codebook if needed
→
Re-code with updated codebook
Code independently first, always. If coders discuss before coding, you no longer have two independent perspectives to reconcile — just one opinion, agreed to twice.
Every disagreement gets discussed, not voted on or averaged. The goal is to understand why two people read the same text differently — that reason often reveals an ambiguity worth fixing in the codebook itself.
A third coder or senior reviewer breaks persistent ties. Some disagreements won't resolve through discussion alone — decide in advance who has the final call, and document it.
Report your process, not just your final numbers. "Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when consensus wasn't reached" is a real, checkable methods sentence.

Measuring Agreement: Cohen's Kappa

Simple percent agreement (how often two coders picked the same code) is easy to compute but misleading — two coders will agree by pure chance fairly often, especially with a small codebook. Cohen's kappa corrects for that chance agreement, which is why it's the standard reported statistic.

Most journals expect at least substantial agreement (κ ≥ 0.61) on a final codebook before a single coder proceeds through the rest of the dataset alone. A low kappa isn't just reported and moved past — it's a signal to go back and revise the codebook's definitions.
