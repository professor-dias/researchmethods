Structure the Section by Theme, Not by Participant

A qualitative results section is organized the same way your analysis was: one theme at a time, not one participant at a time. For each theme, the pattern is consistent — name it, explain it in your own words, then let participants' own words demonstrate it.

1. Give the theme a heading — the claim-style name from 5.2.4, not a vague topic label.
2. Explain the theme in a few sentences of your own analytic voice — what it means, how widely it appeared, and where it connects to other themes.
3. Support it with two or three exemplar quotes — not to prove the theme exists (your analysis already established that), but to let the reader hear it directly.
4. Move to the next theme, repeating the pattern.

A Quote Has to Do Work, Not Just Decorate

Without a Quote
"Several participants described disengaging from care after their concerns went unaddressed by providers."
Correct, but the reader is taking your word for it — they never hear it in a participant's own voice.
With a Quote
"Several participants described disengaging from care after their concerns went unaddressed by providers: 'By the third appointment I stopped bringing it up. Nobody wrote it down, nobody followed up. I just started managing it myself' (P07)."
Now the reader can judge the interpretation against the actual evidence — and the specificity ("third appointment," "nobody wrote it down") is exactly the texture a summary sentence loses.

Choosing Quotes That Are Actually Representative

The single easiest way to accidentally mislead a reader in qualitative writing: quietly picking the two most vivid, articulate quotes and running them under every theme, regardless of how common that view really was.

👤
Spread Across Participants
Draw exemplar quotes from different participants across your sample, not the same one or two "best talkers" every time.
Watch for: the same participant ID appearing under every theme
⚖️
Show Range, Not Just Agreement
If a theme had a strong majority view and a real minority view, include a quote representing each — not just the version that best fits your narrative.
Watch for: only ever quoting confirming, never disconfirming, cases
✂️
Edit Only for Clarity
Trimming filler ("um," false starts) for readability is fine. Changing the meaning, or removing context that complicates it, is not.
Watch for: an edited quote that reads too cleanly to be real speech
🔒
Protect Identity
Attribute with a pseudonym or ID (e.g., "P07"), never a real name — and check that a distinctive quote combined with demographics doesn't make someone identifiable anyway.
Watch for: small samples where "a 34-year-old rural nurse" is one specific, identifiable person

Standard convention exists for a reason: it lets a reader see exactly where you touched the original words.
