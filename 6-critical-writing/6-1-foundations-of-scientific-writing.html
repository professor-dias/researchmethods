Publishing Is the Last Step of the Research Process, Not a Separate One

Back in 2.1, step nine of the nine-step roadmap was "share the map with everyone else." This entire topic is that one step, unpacked. A rigorous study that never gets written up and shared has, for all practical purposes, not yet finished being research.

A published paper is the "currency" researchers are judged by — but the anxiety around "publish or perish" often comes from treating writing as a separate, intimidating skill from the research itself. It isn't. Academic tradition has settled on a fairly predictable structure for reporting findings, and once you know that structure, writing becomes a matter of filling in a known shape with your own study's content — which is exactly what 6.2 walks through.

One of the best ways to get better at scientific writing has nothing to do with writing: read published articles regularly. Good readers develop an ear for what works, and the 2.4 critique checklists you already built are the exact same skill you'll need to evaluate your own drafts.

Two Different Kinds of "Good"

It's worth separating two things that are easy to conflate: whether a paper's content is rigorous (design, sampling, analysis — the 2.4 critique checklists) and whether its writing is good. A methodologically flawless study can still be rejected for being poorly written, and a beautifully written paper can't rescue a flawed design. This topic is entirely about the second half.

✍️
Concise
Every sentence earns its place. Journal space is limited; padding gets cut by an editor if you don't cut it yourself.
🎯
Parsimonious
The simplest explanation that fits the data — don't reach for a complicated account when a simple one works.
📚
Well Organized
A reader should never have to guess what section they're in or why a sentence appears where it does.
🔬
Logical
Each claim follows from what came before it — no unexplained leaps.
🚫
Jargon-Free
Technical terms only where they add precision, always defined on first use.
⚖️
Free of Biased Language
Neutral, precise language about people and findings — description, not editorializing.

Finding the Right Journal

Before you write a word, three questions decide where the manuscript is headed:

Does the journal match your topic and audience? A finding about clinic-based counseling reaches the right readers in a clinical journal; a finding about community-level policy reaches the right readers somewhere else entirely.
Will your intended readers understand the analysis? Health research is multidisciplinary — psychologists, epidemiologists, and clinicians don't all "speak the same statistical language." Match the journal to who needs to act on your findings.
What's the journal's impact factor? A rough proxy for how influential a journal is in its field — useful context, but not the only factor in fit.
Impact factor, concretely: a journal's 2013 impact factor = (citations in 2013 to articles it published in 2011–2012) ÷ (number of citable articles it published in 2011–2012). It's an average, so it can range enormously — from the low single digits for a solid field-specific journal to 50+ for a top-tier general medical journal. A high impact factor isn't the goal by itself; a well-matched audience for your specific finding is.

Once you've picked a journal, find its "Instructions for Authors" (sometimes called "Author Guidelines") and follow it precisely — word limits, structure, and citation style are usually non-negotiable, and they change often enough that it's worth re-checking right before you write, not just once at the start of a project.
