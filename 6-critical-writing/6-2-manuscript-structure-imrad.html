One Shape, Filled In Every Time

IMRaD — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — is the near-universal skeleton of a quantitative research manuscript, wrapped by an Abstract at the front and a Reference list at the back. Every section has a specific, non-overlapping job. Once you internalize what belongs where, "what do I write next" stops being a blank-page problem and becomes a fill-in-the-structure problem.

What Each Section Is For

Open each section below for what it needs to contain — use this as a checklist while drafting, not something to read start to finish in one sitting.

Abstract — your one chance to be read at all 

Usually 250 words or fewer, and often the only part of the paper most readers ever see — it has to work as a complete, stand-alone story. It should convey: the research question, sample size and sampling technique, participation rate, study design, key measures, headline findings (with test statistics), and a one-sentence conclusion. There's no room for filler phrases like "implications will be discussed" — if it's important enough to mention, it's important enough to actually say.

Introduction — broad to narrow, ending at your question 

An effective introduction has a predictable trajectory: it opens broad, reviews the relevant chain of research, identifies a genuine gap in that chain (2.4's Step 6), and narrows to your specific research question as the logical next link. Cite selectively — one strong review article usually serves a reader better than eight overlapping individual citations. Restraint here signals control of the literature, not a lack of it.

This is literally 2.4's Literature Review, compressed: the same "find the gap" logic, now written as a funnel instead of a synthesis matrix.
Methods — enough detail that a stranger could repeat your study 
Sample
Population, inclusion/exclusion criteria, sampling technique and rationale (3.1.5), participation rate, and — for follow-up studies — retention rate.
Data Collection
How the data were gathered, where, and why that mode fit the research question and population — plus the required sentence confirming IRB approval.
Measures
Each construct, the instrument used to assess it, its psychometric properties if available, and a sample item — organized by predictor variables, then outcome variables.
Data Analysis
The specific statistical techniques used (3.2's toolkit), any variable transformations, and how statistical significance was defined — described once, then followed without exception in Results.
Results — facts only, no interpretation yet 

Two jobs, in order: first, describe who was in the sample (the demographics and descriptive statistics a reader needs for context); then report what you found, in terse, technical language tied directly back to the research question — "Just the facts." Interpretation belongs in Discussion, not here.

Tables vs. figures: tables are the right place for test statistics, confidence intervals, and p-values; figures are more efficient for showing a relationship or trend visually (3.3.2). Either way, the surrounding text should give a reader the bird's-eye view even if they never look at the table — and the table should tell its own complete story even if they skip the paragraph.
Discussion — the one section where you finally get a voice 

This is the first point in the manuscript where interpretation is not only allowed, it's expected — but it still has to stay tethered to what the data actually showed.

Open with a plain-language summary. No statistics, no jargon — a reader skimming just this paragraph should understand what you found.
Situate the findings in the existing literature. Where do they agree with prior work? Where do they diverge, and why might that be?
Explain unsupported hypotheses too, not just the ones that panned out.
Bring it back to practice. Health research earns its keep by being useful to practitioners — say how.
Stay humble. No single study is definitive; frame findings as adding to, not settling, the evidence base.

Limitations deserve their own honest paragraph — reviewers will find them whether you name them or not, so naming them first is a mark of a careful researcher, not a weakness. Run through 2.3's nine threats plus the usual suspects: participation bias, attrition bias, self-report bias (social desirability, recall), sampling limitations, and design limitations.

Conclusions close the paper — two or three sentences, directly answering the research question, free of hedging phrases but also free of overreach ("suggests," not "proves"). A useful test: could this survive being read aloud as a 30-second news summary?

A Worked Example

An annotated abstract, sentence by sentence 

Background: This study examined predictors of sleep quality among night-shift hospital nurses. (States the research question immediately — no throat-clearing.)

Methods: A cross-sectional survey was administered to a convenience sample (n = 214) of registered nurses working rotating night shifts at three urban hospitals. (Design, sample size, sampling technique, and population, all in one sentence.)

Results: Higher perceived workplace social support (β = .28) and lower caffeine consumption after 3 PM (β = −.19) were independently associated with better sleep quality (adjusted R² = .24). (The research question answered with the actual statistics, not a vague summary.)

Conclusion: Sleep quality among night-shift nurses may be improved by workplace support structures and caffeine-timing guidance, though the cross-sectional design limits causal claims. (Practical, appropriately hedged, and honest about the design's limits — in one sentence.)
