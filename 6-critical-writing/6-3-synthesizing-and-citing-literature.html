You've Already Synthesized. Now You Have to Write It.

2.4's synthesis matrix was for you — a private tool to find the gap in the literature. This page is about turning that same synthesis into prose a reader can follow, plus the citation mechanics that make it defensible.

The single most common failure mode in student writing is a paragraph that summarizes sources one at a time: "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2021) found Y. Lee (2022) found Z." That's a list, not an argument. A synthesized paragraph is organized by idea, with sources cited in support of each idea — sometimes several sources backing one sentence, exactly as a synthesis matrix already showed you they could.

Summarized (Weak)Synthesized (Strong)
"Smith (2019) found social support predicted adherence. Jones (2021) also found social support mattered. Lee (2022) found a weaker effect.""Social support consistently predicts medication adherence, though the strength of that relationship varies by population (Smith, 2019; Jones, 2021; Lee, 2022)."

Citation Is a Tool, Not a Word-Count Filler

Cite what's load-bearing. A good review article or meta-analysis, cited once, often serves a reader better than ten narrower studies cited in a row.
Cite what's current. Findings from a decade ago may no longer describe the field accurately — prioritize recent work unless you're citing something foundational.
Quality over quantity. A long reference list doesn't make an argument stronger; a well-chosen one does.
A quick gut check before adding any citation: if a reader removed this one source, would the sentence still make its point? If yes, ask whether it needs to be there at all.

Paraphrasing, Not Copying

Reading a source closely and then explaining its finding in your own words and sentence structure is paraphrasing. Changing a few words while keeping the original's structure and phrasing intact is not — it's a form of plagiarism, even with a citation attached, and even when unintentional.

Too Close to the Source
"Adolescents' lack of self-esteem has been associated with diverse health-compromising behaviors such as alcohol and cigarette use." (lightly reworded from the original)
Genuinely Paraphrased
"Low self-esteem in adolescence has repeatedly been linked to risk behaviors, including substance use, in this literature (Author, Year)."

A reliable test: close the source, write the point from memory in your own words, then check your paraphrase against the original only to verify accuracy — never draft with the original text open next to you.

When a Direct Quote Actually Earns Its Place

In quantitative writing, direct quotes are rare — nearly everything gets paraphrased and cited. In qualitative writing, it's the opposite: participant quotes are often required, not optional (5.3.1). The distinction is what's being quoted: quoting another researcher's prose is usually unnecessary; quoting a participant's own words as evidence for a theme is often the whole point.
