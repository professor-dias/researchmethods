Don't Read Start to Finish — Read to Interrogate

This page is where 1.2's inductive/deductive reasoning and 1.1's fallacies stop being abstract philosophy and become a working reading habit.

Reading a paper front-to-back, in order, is usually the slowest and least effective way to evaluate it. Experienced readers jump around on purpose:

1. Abstract first. What's the claim? Is it something you'd expect to care about?
2. Then the Discussion's conclusion. What does the author actually claim to have shown — and does it match the abstract?
3. Then Methods. Does the design (2.2) actually support a claim that strong? This is where the 2.4 critique checklists do their work.
4. Only then, Results and the full Introduction. Now you're reading to check the details, not to discover the story for the first time.

The Anatomy of a Research Argument

Every research argument — the author's, or the one you're about to write — has three parts, whether or not it says so explicitly.

Claim
What the author wants you to believe. Usually the conclusion (6.2).
Evidence
The data that supports it — the actual results (6.2), not a restatement of the claim in different words.
Warrant
The unstated logical bridge connecting evidence to claim — often the design (2.2) and the assumption that no rival explanation (2.3) fits better.
Most weak arguments aren't wrong about the evidence — they're weak in the warrant. "Screen time correlates with anxiety, therefore screen time causes anxiety" has real evidence and a real claim; the warrant — that no confounder or reverse-causation story fits better — is exactly what a cross-sectional design (2.2) can't support. That's 1.1's Correlation ≠ Causation fallacy, showing up again in argument form.

Building Your Own Argument on Purpose

State the claim you can actually defend — not the one you wish your data supported. If the design only supports "associated with," don't write "causes."
Show the evidence before the interpretation. Let the reader see the result, then explain what you think it means — not the reverse.
Make the warrant explicit when it's not obvious. If your design rules out a specific rival explanation, say so directly rather than assuming the reader will infer it.
Address the obvious counterargument yourself. A reviewer will think of the rival explanation whether you mention it or not (6.5) — naming and addressing it first is stronger than hoping no one asks.
