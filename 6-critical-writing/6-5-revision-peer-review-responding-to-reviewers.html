What Actually Happens After You Hit Submit

A manuscript rarely goes straight from submission to publication. Knowing the real pipeline in advance makes the process feel like a known procedure instead of a black box.

Submission: The editor may screen it before it's even sent to reviewers — a paper that's obviously out of scope can be rejected here, fast.
Peer review: Typically two or more reviewers with relevant expertise, usually anonymous to you. Their comments go back to the editor, not directly to you.
Editor's decision: Accept, accept with minor contingencies, invite a revised version ("revise and resubmit"), or reject. Revise-and-resubmit is not a rejection — it's the most common path to acceptance for a solid paper.
Revision: You address every reviewer comment and resubmit — sometimes to the same reviewers for a second look.
Galleys & publication: Once accepted, you'll proofread a final typeset version before it's officially published and indexed.

Reading an Editor's Decision Correctly

DecisionWhat It Actually Means
AcceptRare on a first submission — the paper needs no further changes.
Accept with minor revisionsSmall, usually clarity-level fixes. The paper is essentially accepted.
Major revision / revise & resubmitSubstantive concerns, but the editor sees a viable path to acceptance. Treat this as good news — most published papers passed through here.
RejectNot always about quality — sometimes it's about fit. A rejected paper often finds a home at a better-matched journal (6.1) with the same findings.

Writing a Response Letter That Works

A response to reviewers is itself a piece of persuasive writing — and it follows a predictable, low-drama format.

Address every single comment, point by point. Quote or number each reviewer comment, followed immediately by your response and, if applicable, exactly where the manuscript changed.
Thank the reviewer, even for comments you'll push back on. They spent real time on your work — a defensive tone reads poorly to an editor regardless of who's technically right.
It's fine to disagree — respectfully, with reasons. "We appreciate this point, but respectfully disagree because..." is a completely normal, professional response when you have a genuine methodological reason.
When they're right, say so plainly and fix it. "The reviewer is correct; we have revised the Methods section to clarify..." costs you nothing and builds trust for the rest of the letter.
A reviewer comment is nearly always a gift in disguise: if a trained reader in your field misunderstood something, general readers will too. Treat confusion as a writing problem to fix, not a reader's failure to understand.

The Mindset That Makes Revision Faster

Reviewers aren't adversaries. They're unpaid volunteers trying to make your paper better and protect the field's evidence base — including from your own blind spots.
No study is perfect, and reviewers know that. The bar isn't flawlessness; it's an honest account of what the study can and can't support (6.2's Limitations).
A rejection is information, not a verdict on you. Rejected-then-revised-then-published-elsewhere is the normal life cycle of a great deal of good research.
