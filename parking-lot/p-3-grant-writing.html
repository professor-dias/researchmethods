A Learnable Skill, Not a Talent

Most researchers do not get funded on their first grant proposal. Grant writing is closer to a practiced skill like surfing or piano than an innate talent — it improves with iteration, and rejection is the normal first draft of a funded career, not a signal to stop. The template below is built around the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) format, but the underlying structure — make the case, show the plan, prove you can execute it — applies broadly.

What Reviewers Are Actually Scoring

NIH reviewers score each application on five criteria, 1 (exceptional) to 9 (poor), which together inform an overall impact score from 10 to 90 — lower is better on both scales.

🎯
Significance
Does this address an important problem? How would achieving the aims move the field or practice forward?
🛠️
Approach
Is the design, method, and analysis well-reasoned and appropriate? Flagged as the single most influential factor on the final score.
💡
Innovation
Does it challenge an existing paradigm, or use a genuinely novel concept, method, or tool?
👤
Investigator(s)
Is the team appropriately trained and suited to this specific work?
🏢
Environment
Does the institutional setting — resources, populations, collaborations — support success?
An application doesn't need to be the strongest possible on every single criterion — reviewers weigh them as appropriate for that specific study. Work that's essential but not especially novel can still score well if the Approach and Significance are strong.

The Application, Section by Section

Specific Aims (1 page) — write this last 

Your sales pitch, and the section most reviewers read first and remember longest — but paradoxically the one you should draft last, once the full plan exists to summarize. Five elements: the problem, why your approach solves it, a brief description of the approach, the specific aims and hypotheses, and the long-term significance for the field.

A common first-time mistake: treating the aims/hypotheses as the only required element. Reviewers are reading for all five — the problem and the significance carry real weight too.
Significance (2–3 pages) — a targeted case, not a literature dump 

This is not the place for an exhaustive review (2.4's synthesis matrix already happened elsewhere) — it's a focused argument for why this specific study matters now. Build toward a genuine rationale, and don't be afraid to end with real conviction about the contribution — reviewers often remember how a section ends.

Innovation (≤1 page) — new isn't better if it isn't feasible 

Innovation only helps your score if the Approach section convinces reviewers the innovative piece is actually achievable. A brilliant but implausible method scores worse than a solid, modestly novel one.

Approach (8–9 pages) — the largest section, organized in three phases 
Phase I — Program Development: Hire and train staff, run formative research, build the theoretical framework, develop and pilot-test the intervention and the data-collection instruments.
Phase II — Program Implementation: Recruitment and sampling (3.1.5), randomization procedures, the control condition, quality-assurance checks for intervention fidelity, and retention strategy.
Phase III — Program Evaluation: The data analytic plan matched to each aim, a power analysis (with target sample size inflated for expected attrition — e.g., needing 800 analyzable cases at 20% expected attrition means recruiting 1,000), and the project timeline.

Also specify precise eligibility criteria — inclusion and exclusion — tailored to your aims, not a generic list.

Common First-Time Pitfalls

Writing Specific Aims first, before the plan is fully worked out — write it last.
Turning Significance into an exhaustive literature review instead of a targeted argument.
Inflating the budget to hit the maximum allowed, or underestimating it so tightly the aims can't actually be completed — both read poorly to reviewers.
Proposing an activity without naming who on the team has the skill to actually execute it.
Forgetting to inflate the recruitment target to account for expected attrition in the power analysis.
Skipping pilot testing of the intervention or the data-collection instrument to save time — almost always a false economy.
